1 What Is Cluster Sampling?

Textbook sections:

  • 5.1–5.3 in Lohr
  • Ch 8 and 9 in Scheaffer et al.

An Example of Clusters

Suppose we want to find out how many bicycles are owned by residents in a community of 10,000 households. We could take an SRS of 400 households, or we could divide the community into blocks of about 20 households each and sample every household (or subsample some households) in each of 20 blocks selected at random from the 500 blocks in the community.

The latter plan is an example of cluster sampling. The blocks are the primary sampling units (psus), or clusters. The households are the secondary sampling units (ssus); often the ssus are the elements of the population.

Why Do We Use Cluster Sampling?

  • Constructing a sampling frame of observation units may be difficult, expensive, or impossible. We cannot list all honeybees in a region or all customers of a store; a list of individuals in a city may be available only through a list of housing units, which is expensive to build.
  • The population may be widely spread out geographically, or occur in natural clusters such as households or schools, and it is cheaper to sample clusters than to take an SRS of individuals. To survey nursing-home residents, it is much cheaper to sample nursing homes and interview every resident there than to take an SRS of residents.

2 Notation

The Population: Clusters of Cells

Figure 3

y_{ij} = measurement on the jth ssu (cell) in the ith psu (column); M_i = number of ssus in psu i (bottom); t_i = \sum_{j=1}^{M_i} y_{ij} = total of psu i (top). N is the number of psus, not of elements.

psu-Level Population Quantities

t_i = \sum_{j=1}^{M_i} y_{ij} \quad(\text{psu total}), \qquad t = \sum_{i=1}^N t_i \quad(\text{population total}) M_0 = \sum_{i=1}^N M_i \quad(\text{number of ssus in the population}) S_t^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N \Big(t_i - \frac tN\Big)^2 \quad(\text{population variance of psu totals})

ssu-Level Population Quantities

\bar y_U = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^{M_i} y_{ij}}{M_0} = \frac{t}{M_0} \quad(\text{population mean per ssu}) \bar y_{iU} = \frac{t_i}{M_i} \quad(\text{population mean in psu } i) S^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^{M_i}(y_{ij}-\bar y_U)^2}{M_0-1}, \qquad S_i^2 = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M_i}(y_{ij}-\bar y_{iU})^2}{M_i-1}

The Sample

Figure 4

Stage 1: an SRS \mathcal S of n psus (shaded). Stage 2: an SRS \mathcal S_i of m_i of the M_i ssus in each sampled psu (red). One-stage: m_i = M_i. Two-stage: m_i < M_i.

Sample Quantities

From the subsample in psu i: \bar y_i = \frac{1}{m_i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal S_i} y_{ij}, \qquad \hat t_i = \sum_{j\in\mathcal S_i} \frac{M_i}{m_i}y_{ij} = M_i\bar y_i

From the n sampled psus: \bar t = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} \hat t_i, \qquad s_t^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} \big(\hat t_i - \bar t\big)^2

In one-stage sampling \hat t_i = t_i, the true psu total.

Sampling Weights

\begin{aligned} P(j\text{th ssu of }i\text{th psu selected}) &= P(i\text{th psu selected})\times P(j\text{th ssu}\mid i\text{th psu}) \\[4pt] &= \frac nN \cdot \frac{m_i}{M_i} \end{aligned}

So the sampling weight is w_{ij} = \frac{N}{n}\cdot\frac{M_i}{m_i}.

If psus are blocks and ssus are households, household j in block i represents NM_i/(nm_i) households in the population.

3 One-Stage Cluster Sampling with Equal Sizes

Treat the psu Totals as an SRS

Every psu has M_i = M ssus, and all are observed (m_i = M). The psu totals t_i are known for the sampled psus, so we have an SRS of n psu totals and the SRS formulas apply: \hat t = N\bar t = \frac Nn \sum_{i\in\mathcal S} t_i, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat t) = N\sqrt{\Big(1-\frac nN\Big)\frac{s_t^2}{n}}

To estimate \bar y_U, divide by the number of elements NM: \hat{\bar y} = \frac{\hat t}{NM} = \frac{\bar t}{M}, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat{\bar y}) = \frac{1}{M}\sqrt{\Big(1-\frac nN\Big)\frac{s_t^2}{n}}

Example: GPA in a Dormitory

A dorm has N=100 suites of M=4 students. A student selects n=5 suites at random and asks every person in them for their GPA:

Example: GPA Calculations

\begin{aligned} \hat t &= \frac{100}{5}(12.16+11.36+8.96+12.96+11.08) = 1130.4 \\[4pt] s_t^2 &= \frac{1}{4}\Big[(12.16-11.304)^2+\cdots+(11.08-11.304)^2\Big] = 2.256 \\[4pt] \hat{\bar y} &= \frac{1130.4}{400} = 2.826 \\[4pt] \text{SE}(\hat{\bar y}) &= \sqrt{\Big(1-\frac{5}{100}\Big)\frac{2.256}{(5)(4)^2}} = 0.164 \end{aligned}

Note

Treating the nM=20 students as an SRS of the 400 students would give \text{SE} = 0.112, far too small. Whole suites are selected together, and students in the same suite tend to have similar GPAs.

4 Comparing Cluster Sampling with SRS

Population ANOVA Table (Equal Sizes)

Source df Sum of Squares
Between psus N-1 \text{SSB} = \sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^M (\bar y_{iU}-\bar y_U)^2
Within psus N(M-1) \text{SSW} = \sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^M (y_{ij}-\bar y_{iU})^2
Total, about \bar y_U NM-1 \text{SSTO} = \sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^M (y_{ij}-\bar y_U)^2 = (NM-1)S^2

\text{MSB} = \text{SSB}/(N-1), \quad\text{MSW} = \text{SSW}/(N(M-1)).

The Variance Depends on Between-psu Variability

Since t_i - \bar t_U = M(\bar y_{iU}-\bar y_U), S_t^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{(t_i-\bar t_U)^2}{N-1} = M\cdot\text{MSB} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V(\hat t_{\text{cluster}}) = N^2\Big(1-\frac nN\Big)\frac{M\cdot\text{MSB}}{n}

An SRS of the same number nM of elements would give V(\hat t_{\text{SRS}}) = (NM)^2\Big(1-\frac{nM}{NM}\Big)\frac{S^2}{nM} = N^2\Big(1-\frac nN\Big)\frac{MS^2}{n}

So V(\hat t_{\text{cluster}})/V(\hat t_{\text{SRS}}) = \text{MSB}/S^2: if \text{MSB} > S^2, cluster sampling is less efficient than an SRS of the same number of elements.

Intraclass Correlation and Adjusted R^2

Intraclass correlation coefficient: \text{ICC} = 1 - \dfrac{M}{M-1}\cdot\dfrac{\text{SSW}}{\text{SSTO}}

Adjusted R^2 (valid for unequal sizes too): R_a^2 = 1 - \dfrac{\text{MSW}}{S^2}

For equal-sized clusters, \frac{V(\hat t_{\text{cluster}})}{V(\hat t_{\text{SRS}})} = \frac{\text{MSB}}{S^2} = 1 + \frac{N(M-1)}{N-1}R_a^2 \approx 1 + (M-1)R_a^2

So (M-1)R_a^2 is approximately the proportional increase in variance from switching from SRS to cluster sampling. With M=21 and R_a^2=0.05, the variance doubles.

Illustration: R_a^2 Large vs. Small

Figure 5

When cluster means differ a lot relative to within-cluster spread (left), sampling only a few clusters misses most of the population’s variability. This is the mirror image of stratification, where a large R^2 helps.

5 One-Stage Cluster Sampling with Unequal Sizes

The Unbiased Estimator and Its Problem

The unbiased estimator of the total is computed as before: \hat t_{\text{unb}} = \frac Nn \sum_{i\in\mathcal S} t_i, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat t_{\text{unb}}) = N\sqrt{\Big(1-\frac nN\Big)\frac{s_t^2}{n}}

But when the clusters have very different sizes M_i, the totals t_i vary a lot simply because big clusters have more elements. This inflates s_t^2, and hence the variance of \hat t_{\text{unb}}, even before any genuine differences in the y values.

The Ratio Estimator for \bar y_U

Since \bar y_U = t/M_0 and t_i is roughly proportional to M_i, estimating \bar y_U is ratio estimation with y_i = t_i and x_i = M_i: \hat{\bar y}_r = \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} t_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} M_i} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal S} \frac{M_i}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal S} M_k}\,\bar y_i \text{SE}(\hat{\bar y}_r) = \frac{1}{\bar M}\sqrt{\Big(1-\frac nN\Big)\frac{s_e^2}{n}}, \qquad s_e^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}e_i^2, \quad e_i = t_i-\hat{\bar y}_r M_i where \bar M=\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}M_i/n is the average sampled cluster size (M_0 is not needed).

  • \hat{\bar y}_r is a weighted mean of the cluster means \bar y_i (the ssu-level means), with weights proportional to the cluster sizes M_i.
  • s_e^2 is on the scale of the cluster totals: \sqrt{(1-\frac nN)s_e^2/n} is the SE for the mean total per cluster, \bar t. For the mean per ssu, we divide by the average cluster size \bar M.

Why the Ratio Estimator Is Better

Figure 6

The unbiased estimator’s SE depends on the spread of t_i about the horizontal line \bar t (blue), which is large because the sizes vary. The ratio estimator’s SE depends only on the residuals e_i about the line t = \hat{\bar y}_r M (dotted), which are much smaller.

Example: Algebra Class Test Scores

One-stage cluster samples are common in educational studies, since students are naturally clustered into classrooms. From a population of 187 high-school algebra classes, an investigator takes an SRS of n=12 classes and tests every student in them for function knowledge. The classes are the psus, of different sizes M_i.

6 Two-Stage Cluster Sampling

What Is Two-Stage Cluster Sampling?

When observing every ssu in a selected psu is too costly (and elements within a psu are similar), we subsample:

  1. Select an SRS \mathcal S of n psus from the N psus.
  2. Select an SRS \mathcal S_i of m_i ssus from each selected psu i.

Since we no longer observe every ssu in a sampled psu, we estimate each psu total: \hat t_i = \sum_{j\in\mathcal S_i} \frac{M_i}{m_i}y_{ij} = M_i\bar y_i

The Ratio Estimator

As in one-stage sampling, \bar y_U is estimated by a ratio estimator, now with the estimated psu totals \hat t_i: \hat{\bar y}_r = \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\hat t_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}M_i} = \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}M_i\bar y_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}M_i} Its estimated variance is a weighted sum of two variances, with weights 1 - \frac nN and \frac nN: \hat V(\hat{\bar y}_r) = \Big(1-\frac nN\Big)\,\hat V_{\text{ratio}} + \frac nN\,\hat V_{\text{str}}, \hat V_{\text{ratio}} = \frac{s_e^2}{n\bar M^2}, \qquad \hat V_{\text{str}} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal S}\Big(\frac{M_i}{M}\Big)^2\Big(1-\frac{m_i}{M_i}\Big)\frac{s_i^2}{m_i}, with e_i = \hat t_i - \hat{\bar y}_r M_i, \;M = \sum_{i\in\mathcal S}M_i = n\bar M, and s_i^2 the sample variance of the m_i elements measured in psu i.

Reading the Two Pieces

  • \hat V_{\text{ratio}} is the variance of a ratio estimate computed from the n estimated psu totals, as if the psus were drawn with replacement (between-psu variability).
  • \hat V_{\text{str}} is the variance of a stratified sample in which the n sampled psus are the strata, of sizes M_i with m_i units sampled (within-psu variability).
  • The weights move from one to the other as the psu sampling fraction n/N grows:
    • N unknown or very large (n/N \to 0): only \hat V_{\text{ratio}} counts.
    • All psus sampled (n = N): only \hat V_{\text{str}} counts; the design is stratified sampling.
    • One-stage (m_i = M_i): \hat V_{\text{str}} = 0, leaving the one-stage formula \big(1-\frac nN\big)\frac{s_e^2}{n\bar M^2}.
