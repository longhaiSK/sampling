7  Unequal Probability Sampling

Author

Longhai Li

Published

November 23, 2026

Unequal probability sampling (UPS) selects units with different probabilities, usually in proportion to a size variable known for every unit in the population. Large units contribute more to a total, so we give them a larger chance of being selected, and correct for this in the estimator. This chapter builds UPS in three steps, each a special case or an extension of the previous one:

  1. The Hansen–Hurwitz (HH) estimator: the selection probabilities \(\psi_i\) are built from an auxiliary variable \(x_i\), and we estimate the total or mean of \(y_i\) (Section 7.2).
  2. One-stage cluster sampling: the units are clusters (psus), \(y_i\) is the cluster total \(t_i\), and \(x_i\) is the cluster size \(M_i\) (Section 7.4).
  3. Two-stage cluster sampling: the cluster total \(t_i\) is not observed, but estimated from a subsample of the cluster (Section 7.5).

Along the way, Section 7.3 compares the HH estimator with the sample average and the ratio estimator computed from a simple random sample of the same size.

Sampling with replacement (UPSWR) is used throughout, since it makes the variance easy to estimate: we make \(n\) independent draws, and on each draw unit \(i\) is selected with probability \(\psi_i\), where \(\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i=1\). A unit may be selected more than once, and it then appears in the sample once per draw. Sampling without replacement is briefly discussed at the end (Section 7.6).

7.2 The Hansen–Hurwitz Estimator

7.2.1 Theory

The population has \(N\) units. Unit \(i\) has a study value \(y_i\), with population total \(t=\sum_{i=1}^N y_i\) and mean \(\bar{y}_U=t/N\), and an auxiliary variable \(x_i>0\) whose total \(t_x=\sum_{i=1}^N x_i\) is known. If \(y_i\) is roughly proportional to \(x_i\) (for example, the number of physicians of a county and its population), we construct the selection probabilities from \(x_i\): \[ \psi_i=\frac{x_i}{t_x}. \]

The ratio of each unit. Define, for every unit of the population, \[ b_i=\frac{y_i}{x_i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N, \] and let \(B=t/t_x=\bar{y}_U/\bar{x}_U\) be the population ratio. Since \(y_i=x_ib_i\), \(B\) is the mean of the \(b_i\) weighted by \(x_i\), \[ B=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N x_ib_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i}, \] and once \(B\) is known, the mean and the total follow, \[ \bar{y}_U=\bar{x}_U\,B,\qquad t=t_x\,B,\qquad \bar{x}_U=\frac{t_x}{N}. \] So estimating \(B\) is enough: we build an estimator \(\hat{B}\) and convert it to \(\hat{\bar{y}}=\bar{x}_U\hat{B}\) and \(\hat{t}=t_x\hat{B}\). Everything below, for HH, the ratio estimator and the sample average, is written in terms of the \(b_i\).

The sample \(\mathcal{R}\) and its observations \(b_j^*\). We make \(n\) independent draws with replacement; on each draw, unit \(i\) is selected with probability \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\). The sample, denoted \(\mathcal{R}\) (not \(\mathcal{S}\)), is the list of the \(n\) draws including repeats: a unit drawn twice appears twice. Let \(b_j^*\) denote the ratio \(b\) of the unit selected on the \(j\)th draw, that is, the \(j\)th observation in \(\mathcal{R}\) (\(j=1,\ldots,n\)). The star reminds us that \(b_j^*\) is indexed by the draw, not by the population unit: \(b_j^*\) is random, while \(b_1,\ldots,b_N\) are fixed numbers.

Sampling distribution of \(b_j^*\). On every draw, \(b_j^*\) equals \(b_i\) exactly when unit \(i\) is selected, so \(b_j^*\) is a discrete random variable with distribution

Unit selected on draw \(j\) \(1\) \(2\) \(\cdots\) \(N\)
Value of \(b_j^*\) \(b_1\) \(b_2\) \(\cdots\) \(b_N\)
Probability \(\psi_1=x_1/t_x\) \(\psi_2=x_2/t_x\) \(\cdots\) \(\psi_N=x_N/t_x\)

(if several units have the same value of \(b_i\), their probabilities add up). Since the draws are made with replacement, the probabilities do not change from draw to draw, and \(b_1^*,\ldots,b_n^*\) are independent and identically distributed (iid) with this distribution. Its mean and variance are \[ E(b_j^*)=\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{x_i}{t_x}\cdot\frac{y_i}{x_i}=\frac{t}{t_x}=B,\qquad S_{b^*}^2=V(b_j^*)=\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i\,(b_i-B)^2 . \] A single draw is therefore an unbiased estimate of \(B\), and it is a precise one when the \(b_i\) are nearly the same for all units, that is, when \(y_i\) is nearly proportional to \(x_i\).

Hansen–Hurwitz estimator of \(B\). The HH estimator is the average of the \(n\) observations in \(\mathcal{R}\), the mean of the ratios: \[ \boxed{\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\bar{b}^*=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n b_j^*} . \] Being the mean of \(n\) iid copies of \(b_j^*\), it is unbiased, and its variance is that of one draw divided by \(n\): \[ E(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}})=B,\qquad V(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac{S_{b^*}^2}{n}=\frac{1}{nN}\sum_{i=1}^N w^x_i\,(b_i-B)^2 , \qquad w^x_i=\frac{x_i}{\bar{x}_U}=N\psi_i . \] Here \(w^x_i\) is the size of unit \(i\) relative to the average, so that \(\psi_i=w^x_i/N\), and the variance is a sum of terms involving only \(w^x_i\) and \(b_i-B\). There is no finite population correction, since the draws are independent. For the same reason, the sample variance of the \(b_j^*\) is an unbiased estimate of \(S_{b^*}^2\), which gives \[ \boxed{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac{s_{b^*}}{\sqrt{n}}},\qquad s_{b^*}^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^n\left(b_j^*-\bar{b}^*\right)^2 . \]

Mean and total. Multiplying by the known \(\bar{x}_U\) and \(t_x\), the estimator, its standard error and its estimated standard error all scale by the same constant: \[ \boxed{\; \begin{aligned} \hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}&=\bar{x}_U\,\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\bar{x}_U\,\bar{b}^*,\qquad &\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}})&=\bar{x}_U\,\frac{S_{b^*}}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad &\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}})&=\bar{x}_U\,\frac{s_{b^*}}{\sqrt{n}},\\[4pt] \hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}&=t_x\,\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}=t_x\,\bar{b}^*,\qquad &\mathrm{SE}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}})&=t_x\,\frac{S_{b^*}}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad &\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}})&=t_x\,\frac{s_{b^*}}{\sqrt{n}} . \end{aligned}\;} \] Section 7.2.4 writes \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) directly in terms of the selection probabilities \(\psi_i\).

Variance of the mean and total. The variances of \(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) and \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) are \(\bar{x}_U^2\) and \(t_x^2\) times \(V(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}})\). The variance is small when all the ratios \(b_i\) are close to \(B\), that is, when \(y_i\) is nearly proportional to \(x_i\).

7.2.2 Computation Example: Revenue from AI Token Usage

An AI company has \(N=6\) enterprise customers. From its usage logs it knows, for every customer, the number of tokens \(x_i\) (in millions) the customer processed last month, but the revenue \(y_i\) (in thousands of dollars) that the customer generated has to be audited from the billing records, which is costly. Revenue is roughly proportional to token usage, so we draw \(n=5\) customers with replacement, with probabilities \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\) proportional to the tokens, and estimate the revenue per million tokens \(B\), the mean revenue per customer \(\bar{y}_U\), and the total revenue \(t\).

Step 2: the HH estimates of the mean and the total. Multiply by the known mean and total of the tokens, \(\bar{x}_U=16.667\) and \(t_x=100\): \[ \hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\bar{x}_U\,\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}=16.667\times 4.8917=81.53,\qquad \hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=t_x\,\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}=100\times 4.8917=489.2. \] The true values are \(B=4.91\), \(\bar{y}_U=81.83\) and \(t=491\).

The estimate \(81.53\) is \(0.17\) estimated standard errors below \(\bar{y}_U=81.83\).

7.2.3 Example: Physicians in U.S. Counties (statepop.csv)

We want to estimate the total number of physicians \(t\) in the US. Because sampling was done with replacement, large counties like Los Angeles occur multiple times in the sample.

The number of physicians is roughly proportional to the population, which is why this is a good size variable:

To avoid tiny numbers we measure the population in thousands of people, so that \(b_j^*\) is the number of physicians per 1,000 people. 7.2.4 The HH Estimator of the Total in Terms of \(\psi_i\)

The selection probabilities need not come from a size variable: any \(\psi_i>0\) with \(\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i=1\) define a UPSWR design. Let \(y_j^*\) and \(\psi_j^*\) be the study value and the selection probability of the unit selected on draw \(j\). Dividing \(y_j^*\) by its selection probability gives an estimate of the total from that draw alone, \[ u_j=\frac{y_j^*}{\psi_j^*},\qquad E(u_j)=\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i\,\frac{y_i}{\psi_i}=\sum_{i=1}^N y_i=t , \] and the \(u_j\) are iid, since the draws are independent. The HH estimator of the total is their average: \[ \boxed{\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\bar u=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{y_j^*}{\psi_j^*},\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac{s_u}{\sqrt n},\qquad s_u^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{y_j^*}{\psi_j^*}-\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}\right)^2}, \] with variance \[ V(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i\left(\frac{y_i}{\psi_i}-t\right)^2 . \] The mean is estimated by \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}/N\). A unit with a small \(\psi_i\) is rarely drawn, but when it is, its value is multiplied by the large factor \(1/\psi_i\); this is what makes \(u_j\) unbiased. This form is what the function upswr_total computes.

Equivalence with \(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}\). The formula is the estimator of Section 7.2.1 with the selection probabilities themselves as the size variable. Take the normalized size \[ \tilde{x}_i=\psi_i,\qquad\text{so that}\qquad t_{\tilde{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^N\tilde{x}_i=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{\tilde{x}_i}{t_{\tilde{x}}}=\psi_i . \] The ratios and the population ratio for this size variable are \[ \tilde{b}_i=\frac{y_i}{\tilde{x}_i}=\frac{y_i}{\psi_i},\qquad \tilde{B}=\frac{t}{t_{\tilde{x}}}=t , \] so each observation \(\tilde{b}_j^*=y_j^*/\psi_j^*=u_j\), and \[ \hat{\tilde{B}}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\tilde{b}_j^*=\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}},\qquad \hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=t_{\tilde{x}}\,\hat{\tilde{B}}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\hat{\tilde{B}}_{\mathrm{HH}},\qquad s_{\tilde{b}^*}=s_u . \] With a normalized size, the “ratio” \(\tilde{B}\) is the total, and the estimator, its SE and its variance are those of Section 7.2.1. Conversely, when \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\), every draw gives \(y_j^*/\psi_j^*=t_x\,y_j^*/x_j^*=t_x\,b_j^*\), so \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=t_x\,\bar{b}^*\) and \(s_u=t_x\,s_{b^*}\), the formulas for the total in Section 7.2.1. Rescaling a size variable to total 1 therefore does not change the estimator of the total, since \(\psi_i\) depends on \(x_i\) only through \(x_i/t_x\).

7.3 Comparison of the HH, Ratio, and Average Estimators of the Total

The HH estimator uses the size variable \(x_i\) at the design stage, through the selection probabilities \(\psi_i\). With a simple random sample, the same information can instead be used at the estimation stage, by the ratio estimator, or ignored, by the sample average. This section compares the three estimators of the total \(t\): their variances, a toy example in which each of them can win, a simulation with the agpop data, and an app.

7.3.1 Theory

The sample average and the ratio estimator use a simple random sample (SRS) \(\mathcal{S}\) of \(n\) units drawn without replacement; HH uses \(n\) draws with replacement, with selection probabilities \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\). The ratio estimator and HH are best compared through the residuals about the line through the origin, \[ d_i=y_i-Bx_i,\qquad B=\frac{t}{t_x},\qquad \sum_{i=1}^N d_i=t-Bt_x=0 . \]

  • The sample average ignores \(x_i\): \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{avg}}=N\bar{y}\), with the SRS variance \[ V(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{avg}})=\left(1-\frac nN\right)\frac{N^2S_y^2}{n},\qquad S_y^2=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\left(y_i-\bar{y}_U\right)^2 . \]
  • The ratio estimator (Section 5.3.1) is \(\hat{t}_r=t_x\,\bar{y}/\bar{x}\), and its (large-\(n\)) variance is the SRS variance of the residuals, \(\left(1-\frac nN\right)N^2S_d^2/n\) with \(S_d^2=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N d_i^2\). Since an SRS gives every unit the same selection probability \(1/N\), it can be written in the same form as the HH variance below: \[ V(\hat{t}_r)\approx\left(1-\frac nN\right)\frac{N}{N-1}\cdot\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{d_i^2}{1/N} \approx\left(1-\frac nN\right)\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{d_i^2}{1/N}. \]
  • The HH estimator is \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n y_j^*/\psi_j^*\) (Section 7.2.4), with variance \(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i\left(y_i/\psi_i-t\right)^2\). With \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\), each one-draw deviation is a scaled residual, \[ \frac{y_i}{\psi_i}-t=\frac{t_x}{x_i}\left(y_i-\frac{t}{t_x}\,x_i\right)=\frac{d_i}{\psi_i}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad V(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{d_i^2}{\psi_i}. \]

For the mean, divide the estimators by \(N\) and the variances by \(N^2\); for \(B=t/t_x\), divide by \(t_x\) and \(t_x^2\).

On a common scale. The ratio and HH variances differ only in the selection probability that divides \(d_i^2\): \(1/N\) for every unit in an SRS, \(\psi_i\) under UPSWR. Writing the sample average the same way, \(N^2S_y^2/n\approx\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^N(y_i-\bar{y}_U)^2/(1/N)\), and ignoring the finite population correction, each variance is \(\frac1n\) times a sum over the units of one term, a squared deviation divided by the unit’s selection probability:

Estimator Term of unit \(i\) Same, as \(N\times\) (squared deviation) / (relative size) Spread of \(y\) about
Sample average \(\dfrac{(y_i-\bar{y}_U)^2}{1/N}\) \(\dfrac{N(y_i-\bar{y}_U)^2}{1}\) the flat line \(\bar{y}_U\)
Ratio \(\dfrac{d_i^2}{1/N}\) \(\dfrac{N\,d_i^2}{1}\) the line \(y=Bx\)
HH \(\dfrac{d_i^2}{\psi_i}\) \(\dfrac{N\,d_i^2}{x_i/\bar{x}_U}=\dfrac{N\,d_i^2}{w^x_i}\) the line \(y=Bx\)

Because \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x=\frac1N\,x_i/\bar{x}_U\), the last column shows the contrast most directly: the three terms share the factor \(N\) and differ only in what divides the squared deviation, namely \(1\) for the two SRS estimators and the relative size \(w^x_i=x_i/\bar{x}_U\) for HH.

Ratio versus average. Since \(y_i-\bar{y}_U=d_i+B(x_i-\bar{x}_U)\), the two terms differ by \(B(x_i-\bar{x}_U)\). When \(y_i\) is roughly proportional to \(x_i\), the residuals \(d_i\) are small and the spread of \(y\) comes mostly from that of \(x\), which the ratio estimator removes. When \(y_i\) is not related to \(x_i\), the residual \(d_i=(y_i-\bar{y}_U)-B(x_i-\bar{x}_U)\) adds the spread of \(x\) to that of \(y\), and the sample average does better.

HH versus ratio. Both use the same squared residuals \(d_i^2\); they differ only in what they are divided by, the equal probability \(1/N\) for the ratio estimator and \(\psi_i\) for HH. A unit larger than average (\(\psi_i>1/N\)) has its residual downweighted under HH, and a smaller one upweighted. The reason is that HH gives equal weights to the draws because the large units are already drawn more often, whereas the SRS estimators give every unit the same chance. UPSWR therefore wins when the large residuals \(d_i\) belong to the large units, that is, when the spread of \(y_i\) about the line grows with \(x_i\), as it does for county acreages. When the spread of \(y_i\) is the same for all \(x_i\), the SRS ratio estimator does better, and when \(y_i\) is not related to \(x_i\) at all, all the \(d_i\) are large and both can be worse than the plain sample average. The toy example in Section 7.3.2 illustrates these cases.

When does UPS beat the ratio estimator? Ignoring the finite population correction, the difference of the two variances is a single sum, \[ V(\hat{t}_r)-V(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}})\approx\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^N d_i^2\left(N-\frac{1}{\psi_i}\right) =\frac Nn\sum_{i=1}^N d_i^2\left(1-\frac{\bar{x}_U}{x_i}\right), \] so HH has the smaller variance exactly when \[ \boxed{H_d=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N d_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^N d_i^2/x_i}>\bar{x}_U}, \] that is, when the harmonic mean of the sizes, weighted by the squared residuals, exceeds their ordinary mean: the units with large residuals are larger than average. In terms of the features of the population, suppose the spread of \(y\) about the line \(y=Bx\) grows like a power of the size, \(d_i^2\approx\sigma^2x_i^{\gamma}\). Then the sum above is proportional to \(\sum_i x_i^{\gamma-1}(x_i-\bar{x}_U)\), a multiple of the covariance of \(x_i^{\gamma-1}\) with \(x_i\), whose sign is that of \(\gamma-1\):

Spread of \(y\) about the line \(y=Bx\) \(\gamma\) Better estimator
Constant: \(\mathrm{SD}(d_i)\) does not depend on \(x_i\) \(0\) ratio (SRS)
\(\mathrm{SD}(d_i)\propto\sqrt{x_i}\): \(V(y_i)\propto x_i\), as for sums of \(x_i\) independent pieces \(1\) tie
\(\mathrm{SD}(d_i)\propto x_i\): a constant coefficient of variation, \(\mathrm{SD}(y_i)\propto Bx_i\) \(2\) HH (UPSWR)

Figure 7.2 shows the three cases for one skewed population of sizes, with the same line \(y=Bx\) and the same average spread about it; only the way the spread grows with \(x\) differs.

Many skewed populations with a natural size, such as revenues by customer size, acreages by county area, or counts by population, have a roughly constant coefficient of variation (\(\gamma\approx2\)), which is why PPS works well for them. A plot of \(y\) against \(x\) from a census or a pilot sample shows which case applies: if the scatter about the line fans out in proportion to \(x\), sample with PPS; if its width stays about the same, use an SRS with the ratio estimator. Three further points favour the SRS ratio estimator: its finite population correction \(1-n/N\), which matters when the sampling fraction is large; the repeated units of sampling with replacement (a \(\pi\)ps design without replacement, Section 7.6, removes this); and a line with an intercept, when \(y\) is not proportional to \(x\), where both rely on the wrong line and the regression estimator with an SRS is safer. In the toy example, \(H_d\) is 529 for the first population and 423 for the second, both above \(\bar{x}_U=400\), so HH beats the ratio estimator in both, as the working tables below confirm.

7.4 One-Stage Cluster Sampling with PPS

7.4.1 Theory

In cluster sampling, the units of the HH estimator are clusters (primary sampling units, psus), and the study value of unit \(i\) is the cluster total \(t_i=\sum_{j=1}^{M_i}y_{ij}\). In a one-stage design, every element (secondary sampling unit, ssu) of a selected cluster is observed, so \(t_i\) is known for the sampled clusters. The auxiliary variable is the cluster size \(M_i\), known for all clusters. So the notation of Section 7.2.1 changes as \[ y_i \to t_i,\qquad x_i\to M_i,\qquad t_x\to M_0=\sum_{i=1}^N M_i,\qquad \bar{x}_U\to\bar{M}_U=\frac{M_0}{N}, \qquad \psi_i=\frac{M_i}{M_0}. \] The ratio of cluster \(i\) is \[ b_i=\frac{t_i}{M_i}=\bar{y}_i , \] the mean per element (ssu-level mean) of cluster \(i\), and \(B=t/M_0\) is the mean per element of the whole population, which we denote also by \(\bar{y}_U\). Let \(b_j^*=\bar{y}_j^*\) be the mean per element of the cluster selected on draw \(j\). Then everything in Section 7.2.1 applies, with \(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) written in the cluster notation as \(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\):

\[ \boxed{\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \bar{y}_j^*},\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac{s_{\bar{y}^*}}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad s_{\bar{y}^*}^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^n\left(\bar{y}_j^*-\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\right)^2 . \]

That is, the estimate of the mean per element is the plain average of the means of the sampled clusters: large clusters are selected more often, and each selected cluster is then weighted equally. Multiplying by the known constants gives the estimates of the three quantities of interest, with their estimated SEs multiplied by the same constants:

Quantity Estimate Estimated SE
Mean per element (ssu-level mean), \(\bar{y}_U=B=t/M_0\) \(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) \(s_{\bar{y}^*}/\sqrt{n}\)
Mean per cluster (psu-level mean of the totals), \(\bar{t}_U=t/N\) \(\hat{\bar{t}}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\bar{M}_U\,\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) \(\bar{M}_U\,s_{\bar{y}^*}/\sqrt{n}\)
Total, \(t=\sum_{i=1}^N t_i\) \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=M_0\,\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) \(M_0\,s_{\bar{y}^*}/\sqrt{n}\)

The function cluster_upswr(ti, Mi, N, M0, estimate) computes these estimates. It simply calls upswr_est with \(y=t\) (the cluster totals) and \(x=M\) (the cluster sizes), \(\bar{x}_U=\bar{M}_U\) and \(t_x=M_0\), and reports the working table and the results in the cluster notation; estimate is "ybar" (mean per element), "tbar" (mean per cluster) or "total":

7.5 Two-Stage Cluster Sampling with PPS

7.5.1 Theory

In a two-stage design, we select \(n\) clusters with replacement with probabilities \(\psi_i=M_i/M_0\), and then take a simple random sample of \(m_i\) elements from the \(M_i\) elements of the cluster on every draw. The cluster total \(t_i\) is no longer observed, so we cannot compute the cluster mean \(b_i=t_i/M_i\) of Section 7.4.1. We estimate it from the subsample, \[ \bar{y}_i=\frac{1}{m_i}\sum_{j=1}^{m_i}y_{ij},\qquad \hat{t}_i=M_i\,\bar{y}_i , \] where \(\bar{y}_i\) is the mean of the subsample. The one-stage formulas carry over with the subsample mean \(\bar{y}_j^*\) of draw \(j\) in place of the cluster mean \(b_j^*\): \[ \boxed{\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \bar{y}_j^*},\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac{s_{\bar{y}^*}}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad s_{\bar{y}^*}^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^n\left(\bar{y}_j^*-\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\right)^2 , \] and the estimates of the mean per element, the mean per cluster and the total are \(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\), \(\hat{\bar{t}}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\bar{M}_U\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\) and \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=M_0\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}\), with their SEs multiplied by the same constants, as in the table of Section 7.4.1. Equivalently, each draw estimates the total by \(\hat{t}_j^*/\psi_j^*=M_0\,\bar{y}_j^*\).

There are two differences from the one-stage case. First, a cluster that is drawn twice is subsampled independently on each draw, so the two draws give different subsample means \(\bar{y}_j^*\), and both are kept as separate observations. Second, no second-stage formula is needed for the standard error. The \(n\) draws are independent, and the values \(\bar{y}_j^*\) differ between draws for two reasons, the choice of the clusters and the choice of the elements within them. The sample variance \(s_{\bar{y}^*}^2\) of the values \(\bar{y}_j^*\) captures both, so the variance of the two-stage estimator is computed in exactly the same way as that of the one-stage estimator. This is a major simplification compared with two-stage SRS of clusters, where the variance has a between-cluster and a within-cluster piece. Of course, a larger subsample \(m_i\) makes \(\bar{y}_i\) closer to \(t_i/M_i\) and reduces the variance, but it does so through \(s_{\bar{y}^*}^2\).

Why the within-cluster variation is absorbed into \(V(\bar{y}_j^*)\). Write \(\bar{y}_{iU}=t_i/M_i\) for the true mean of cluster \(i\), \(S_i^2\) for the variance of its \(M_i\) elements, and \(V_{2i}=\left(1-\frac{m_i}{M_i}\right)\frac{S_i^2}{m_i}\) for the variance of the subsample mean \(\bar{y}_i\) of an SRS of \(m_i\) elements. The value \(\bar{y}_j^*\) of draw \(j\) has two sources of randomness: which cluster \(i\) is drawn (with probability \(\psi_i\)), and which \(m_i\) elements are subsampled from it. Conditioning on the cluster, \(E(\bar{y}_j^*\mid i)=\bar{y}_{iU}\) and \(V(\bar{y}_j^*\mid i)=V_{2i}\), so \[ E(\bar{y}_j^*)=\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i\,\bar{y}_{iU}=\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{M_i}{M_0}\frac{t_i}{M_i}=\bar{y}_U, \] and, by the law of total variance, \[ V(\bar{y}_j^*)=\underbrace{V\big(E[\bar{y}_j^*\mid i]\big)}_{\text{which cluster}} +\underbrace{E\big(V[\bar{y}_j^*\mid i]\big)}_{\text{which elements}} =\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i\left(\bar{y}_{iU}-\bar{y}_U\right)^2}_{\text{between clusters}} +\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^N\psi_i\,V_{2i}}_{\text{within clusters}} . \] In one-stage sampling \(V_{2i}=0\) and only the between-cluster term remains. Because the \(n\) draws are independent and identically distributed, so are \(\bar{y}_1^*,\dots,\bar{y}_n^*\), and their sample variance is unbiased for the whole variance: \[ E\left(s_{\bar{y}^*}^2\right)=V(\bar{y}_j^*)=\text{between}+\text{within},\qquad E\left[\widehat{V}(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}})\right]=E\left(\frac{s_{\bar{y}^*}^2}{n}\right)=\frac{V(\bar{y}_j^*)}{n}=V(\hat{\bar{y}}_{\mathrm{HH}}). \] The within-cluster variation is visible in the spread of the values \(\bar{y}_j^*\): two draws of clusters with the same true mean still give different \(\bar{y}_j^*\) because of the subsampling (home H12 in the example below gives \(0.95\) on one draw and \(0.80\) on the other). So no estimate of \(S_i^2\) is needed, and the method works even with \(m_i=1\). This relies on three conditions: the clusters are drawn with replacement, a cluster drawn twice is subsampled again, independently, on each draw (reusing one subsample would make those two draws dependent), and the subsample mean is unbiased for the cluster mean, for example with an SRS within the cluster.

Compare with two-stage sampling of clusters without replacement. There, the cluster-level values are not iid: the between-cluster formula applied to the estimated totals carries the finite population correction \((1-n/N)\), and captures only a fraction (about \(n/N\)) of the within-cluster variance, so the rest must be added back with an estimate of \(S_i^2\). With replacement there is no finite population correction, and nothing is lost. The price is that \(s_{\bar{y}^*}^2\) estimates only the sum of the two components: a single sample does not tell how much of the variance is due to the choice of the clusters and how much to the subsampling, which is what one would need to decide between more clusters and larger subsamples \(m_i\).

7.6 UPS Without Replacement

7.6.1 Review of UPSWR and the Horvitz–Thompson Estimator

Review of UPSWR in terms of \(\psi_i\). With replacement, each of the \(n\) draws selects unit \(i\) with probability \(\psi_i\), and draw \(j\) gives the unbiased estimate \(y_j^*/\psi_j^*\) of the total \(t\), since \(E(y_j^*/\psi_j^*)=\sum_i\psi_i\,y_i/\psi_i=t\). The HH estimator averages the \(n\) estimates, \[ \hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{y_j^*}{\psi_j^*},\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}})=\frac{s_u}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad s_u^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{y_j^*}{\psi_j^*}-\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}\right)^2 , \] where the SE is easy because the \(n\) estimates are iid. With \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\), \(\frac{y_j^*}{\psi_j^*}=t_x\,b_j^*\), which gives the estimator \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HH}}=t_x\bar{b}^*\) of Section 7.2.1. The drawback of UPSWR is that a large unit can be drawn several times, which is wasteful.

Without replacement. Now \(n\) distinct units are selected, and the design is described by the inclusion probabilities \[ \pi_i=P(\text{unit } i\in\mathcal{S}),\qquad \sum_{i=1}^N\pi_i=n , \] for a sample of fixed size \(n\). For a size variable \(x_i\), we take \(\pi_i=n\,x_i/t_x\) (inclusion probability proportional to size, \(\pi\)ps), which is \(n\psi_i\) for \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\). A unit with \(n\,x_i/t_x>1\) is included with certainty, \(\pi_i=1\), and the other probabilities are recomputed from the remaining units.

The Horvitz–Thompson (HT) estimator. Weighting each sampled unit by \(1/\pi_i\) gives \[ \boxed{\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{y_i}{\pi_i}} . \] It has the same form as the HH estimator with \(n\psi_i\) replaced by \(\pi_i\) (the two coincide if no unit is drawn twice). It is unbiased. Let \(I_i=1\) if unit \(i\) is in the sample and \(0\) otherwise, so that \(E(I_i)=P(I_i=1)=\pi_i\). Then \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{i=1}^N I_i\,y_i/\pi_i\), and \[ E(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})=\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{y_i}{\pi_i}\,E(I_i)=\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{y_i}{\pi_i}\,\pi_i=\sum_{i=1}^N y_i=t . \] This needs only \(\pi_i>0\) for all units. The estimator of the mean per unit is \(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}/N\). When the inclusion probabilities are proportional to a size variable \(x_i\), \(\pi_i=n\,x_i/t_x\), the estimator of the ratio \(B=t/t_x\) is, as for UPSWR, the HT total divided by \(t_x\), \[ \hat{B}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\frac{\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}}{t_x}=\frac{1}{t_x}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{y_i}{\pi_i} =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{y_i}{x_i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}b_i , \] the plain average of the ratios \(b_i=y_i/x_i\) of the \(n\) sampled units, with \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HT}})=\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})/t_x\). In cluster sampling, \(y_i=t_i\) and \(x_i=M_i\), so that \(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}/M_0\) is the mean per element (Section 7.4.1).

Variances. For a fixed sample size, with the joint inclusion probabilities \(\pi_{ij}=P(i,j\in\mathcal{S})\), \[ V(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N(\pi_i\pi_j-\pi_{ij}) \left(\frac{y_i}{\pi_i}-\frac{y_j}{\pi_j}\right)^2 \qquad\text{(Sen--Yates--Grundy)}, \] which is estimated unbiasedly by summing over the sampled pairs only, each divided by \(\pi_{ij}\): \[ \hat{V}_{\mathrm{SYG}}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}} \frac{\pi_i\pi_j-\pi_{ij}}{\pi_{ij}}\left(\frac{y_i}{\pi_i}-\frac{y_j}{\pi_j}\right)^2 . \] The joint probabilities \(\pi_{ij}\) are hard to compute for most sampling methods, so approximations that need only \(\pi_i\) are used:

  • Hájek’s approximation (for designs with high entropy), obtained by \(\pi_{ij}\approx\pi_i\pi_j\left[1-\frac{(1-\pi_i)(1-\pi_j)}{\sum_k\pi_k(1-\pi_k)}\right]\) in the SYG estimator: \[ \hat{V}_{\mathrm{H}}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})=\frac{n}{n-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(1-\pi_i) \left(\frac{y_i}{\pi_i}-A\right)^2,\qquad A=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(1-\pi_i)\,y_i/\pi_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(1-\pi_i)} . \]

  • With-replacement approximation: treat the sample as \(n\) draws with \(\psi_i=\pi_i/n\) and use the UPSWR formula, \[ \hat{V}_{\mathrm{WR}}(\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}})=\frac{n}{n-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\frac{y_i}{\pi_i}-\frac{\hat{t}_{\mathrm{HT}}}{n}\right)^2 . \] It ignores the factors \(1-\pi_i\) that account for sampling without replacement, so it overestimates the variance, the more so the larger the \(\pi_i\).

For the mean per unit, divide the estimate and the SE by \(N\); for the ratio \(\hat{B}_{\mathrm{HT}}\), divide by \(t_x\).

7.6.2 Selecting a Sample with Given Inclusion Probabilities

The HT estimator needs only the inclusion probabilities \(\pi_i\), but to use it we must be able to draw a sample without replacement whose inclusion probabilities are exactly the \(\pi_i\) we chose. This is less obvious than it looks, because many different designs have the same \(\pi_i\) (they differ in the joint probabilities \(\pi_{ij}\)), and the most natural procedure does not give them.

Step 1: compute the \(\pi_i\). For a size variable \(x_i\) and a sample of size \(n\), start with \(\pi_i=n\,x_i/t_x\). A unit with \(\pi_i\ge 1\) is taken with certainty, \(\pi_i=1\); remove the certainty units, and recompute \(\pi_i=n'x_i/t_x'\) for the others, with \(n'\) the number of places left and \(t_x'\) the total size of the remaining units; repeat until all \(\pi_i\le1\). The function inclusionprobabilities(x, n) of the R package sampling does this. For example, with \(N=6\) units of sizes \(x=(40,25,15,10,6,4)\), \(t_x=100\) and \(n=3\), unit 1 has \(3\times40/100=1.2>1\), so it is a certainty unit, and the other two places are shared by units 2–6 with \(\pi_i=2x_i/60\):

Code 
library(sampling)

The natural procedure does not work. Drawing \(n\) times with probabilities \(\psi_i=x_i/t_x\), and redrawing whenever a unit already in the sample comes up (draw by draw, without replacement), does not give \(\pi_i=n\psi_i\). For example, with \(N=3\), \(x=(2,1,1)\) and \(n=2\), the target is \(\pi=(1,0.5,0.5)\), but unit 1 is in the sample with probability \(P(\text{1 on draw 1})+P(\text{1 on draw 2})=0.5+2\times0.25\times\frac{0.5}{0.75}=0.833\): when a small unit is drawn first, the large unit only has probability \(2/3\) on the second draw.

Step 2: draw the sample. There are many methods for drawing a fixed-size sample without replacement with any given \(\pi_i\) (with \(0<\pi_i\le1\) and \(\sum_i\pi_i=n\) an integer); see Tillé, Sampling Algorithms (Springer, 2006), for a survey. Two of them are simple and general.

  • Systematic \(\pi\)ps sampling (Madow, 1949). Lay the units along a line in some order, unit \(i\) occupying an interval of length \(\pi_i\), so that the line has length \(\sum_i\pi_i=n\). Draw one random start \(u\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\), and select the units whose intervals contain the \(n\) points \(u,\,u+1,\,\dots,\,u+n-1\). Since the points are spaced 1 apart and an interval has length \(\pi_i\le1\), it contains at most one point, and it contains one with probability exactly \(\pi_i\). This is the same line as for UPSWR (Section 7.2.1), with \(n\) equally spaced points instead of \(n\) independent ones; see
  • The pivotal method (Deville and Tillé, 1998). Process the probabilities two at a time. Take two units \(i\) and \(j\) whose current probabilities \(a=\pi_i\) and \(b=\pi_j\) are strictly between 0 and 1, and let them “fight” for their combined probability \(a+b\):
    • if \(a+b<1\): with probability \(\frac{a}{a+b}\) set \((\pi_i,\pi_j)\leftarrow(a+b,0)\), otherwise \((0,a+b)\);
    • if \(a+b\ge1\): with probability \(\frac{1-b}{2-a-b}\) set \((\pi_i,\pi_j)\leftarrow(1,a+b-1)\), otherwise \((a+b-1,1)\).
    Each step decides at least one of the two units (its probability becomes 0 or 1), keeps the sum \(n\), and keeps the expected value of every probability. For example, if \(a+b<1\), the new \(\pi_i\) has expectation \(\frac{a}{a+b}(a+b)+\frac{b}{a+b}\cdot0=a\), and if \(a+b\ge1\) it has expectation \(\frac{1-b}{2-a-b}\cdot1+\frac{1-a}{2-a-b}(a+b-1)=\frac{a(2-a-b)}{2-a-b}=a\). After at most \(N-1\) steps every probability is 0 or 1, the units with 1 form a sample of size \(n\), and unit \(i\) is in it with probability equal to its original \(\pi_i\).

The pivotal method is an instance of a general procedure, the splitting method of Deville and Tillé (1998): at each step, write the current vector of probabilities as a random choice between two vectors, \(\boldsymbol{\pi}=\lambda\,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}+(1-\lambda)\,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)}\), each with sum \(n\) and components in \([0,1]\), and move to \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}\) with probability \(\lambda\), otherwise to \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)}\). The expected value of every component is kept, and the procedure stops when all components are 0 or 1. Almost all fixed-size methods can be described in this way; they differ in how the vector is split, which determines the joint probabilities \(\pi_{ij}\). ## systematic pi-ps: points u, u+1, ..., u+n-1 on the cumulated pi_i
sys_pps <- function(pik) {
  n <- round(sum(pik))
  cum <- c(0, cumsum(pik))
  u <- runif(1)
  findInterval(u + 0:(n - 1), cum, left.open = TRUE)
}

## pivotal method: two undecided units fight for their combined probability
pivotal_pps <- function(pik, eps = 1e-9) {
  repeat {
    open <- which(pik > eps & pik < 1 - eps)   # undecided units
    if (length(open) < 2) break
    i <- open[1]; j <- open[2]
    a <- pik[i]; b <- pik[j]
    if (a + b < 1) {
      if (runif(1) < a / (a + b)) { pik[i] <- a + b; pik[j] <- 0 }
      else                        { pik[i] <- 0;     pik[j] <- a + b }
    } else {
      if (runif(1) < (1 - b) / (2 - a - b)) { pik[i] <- 1;         pik[j] <- a + b - 1 }
      else                                  { pik[i] <- a + b - 1; pik[j] <- 1 }
    }
  }
  which(pik > 1 - eps)
}

7.7.2 When to Use Unequal Probability Sampling

WarningRemark: UPS is risky unless y is tied to the size variable

The gain from unequal probability sampling depends entirely on how close the ratios \(b_i=y_i/x_i\) are to a constant, that is, on \(y_i\) being roughly proportional to the size variable \(x_i\) used for \(\psi_i\) or \(\pi_i\). Then the residuals \(d_i=y_i-Bx_i\) in Table 7.3 are small, and so is the variance, as for the county acreages. Otherwise,

  • the estimate can be much worse than a plain SRS average, as in the second population of Section 7.3.2, where the sales are unrelated to the floor area;
  • a unit with a small \(x_i\) but a large \(y_i\) gets a very large weight \(1/\psi_i\) (or \(1/\pi_i\)) when it is selected, so the estimates are unstable, and the sample variance of the \(b_j^*\) that estimates the SE tends to be too small, so that the intervals under-cover.

UPS is therefore used mostly when the size is a good proxy of what is measured: selecting the clusters of a multi-stage survey with probabilities proportional to their number of households or students, auditing by the monetary value of the items, or surveying establishments by their employment. When the size is only loosely related to \(y_i\), use simple random or stratified sampling, with a ratio or regression estimator if \(x_i\) is known, which is much safer than using \(x_i\) at the design stage.

7.7.3 Practical Issues of Sampling Without Replacement

Sampling without replacement avoids drawing a large unit twice, but it is harder than UPSWR, in the selection and above all in the analysis.

Selecting the sample is no longer a real difficulty. Software draws a fixed-size sample with given \(\pi_i\) in one line, for example UPbrewer(pik) or UPmaxentropy(pik) in the R package sampling, and systematic \(\pi\)ps sampling is easy to do by hand (Section 7.6.2). The two points that need care are to make the units with \(n\,x_i/t_x\ge1\) certainty units, and not to use the natural “draw with \(\psi_i\) and redraw on duplicates” procedure, which does not give the intended \(\pi_i\).

Estimating the variance is the real cost. The unbiased SYG estimator needs the joint inclusion probabilities \(\pi_{ij}\), which are hard to compute for most designs, and for systematic sampling some \(\pi_{ij}\) are \(0\), so the estimator cannot be computed at all. In practice the \(\pi_{ij}\) are avoided:

  • Small sampling fraction: analyse as with replacement. With and without replacement differ only through factors of about \(1-\pi_i\); when all \(\pi_i\) are small, the with-replacement variance \(\hat V_{\mathrm{WR}}\) is only slightly conservative. This is the standard practice of large surveys and survey software, which treat the first-stage units as drawn with replacement.
  • Some large \(\pi_i\): a high-entropy design with Hájek’s approximation. Maximum entropy (conditional Poisson), Brewer, Sampford, Tillé and randomized systematic sampling have high entropy, and for them the Hájek approximation \(\hat V_{\mathrm{H}}\), which needs only the \(\pi_i\), is accurate. This is the combination used in Section 7.6.3.
  • Avoid plain systematic sampling of a fixed list when a design-based variance estimate is needed; randomize the order of the units first.

Simpler alternatives give most of the benefit of \(\pi\)ps sampling with an easy analysis:

  • UPSWR itself: when \(n/N\) is small, repeated draws are rare and the simple HH analysis of Section 7.2 loses almost nothing.
  • Stratification by size with an SRS within each stratum (and a larger sampling fraction in the strata of large units), analysed with the usual stratified formulas.
  • Poisson sampling: each unit is included independently with probability \(\pi_i\), so \(\pi_{ij}=\pi_i\pi_j\) and \(\hat V(\hat t_{\mathrm{HT}})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}(1-\pi_i)\,y_i^2/\pi_i^2\) is simple, at the price of a random sample size.

In short, sampling without replacement pays off mainly when the sampling fraction is large, so that UPSWR would waste a noticeable part of the sample on repeated draws of the large units.
